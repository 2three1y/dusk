Hi, meet Dusk

Dusk is a free, open-source English (US) voice I'm making for Piper, a neural text-to-speech system that runs completely offline, right on your own device.

I use a screen reader all day, every day, so I wanted a voice that actually feels good to listen to. Here's what I'm going for:

  • Clear. Clean audio that's easy to understand.
  • Lively. Real ups and downs in her pitch, not a flat drone.
  • Quick. Still crisp when you crank the speech rate way up.

Where things stand: she's still training! The first early test version, Dusk Preview 1, is ready for you to download right below. The finished voice isn't out yet, but she's getting there.

What Dusk is

Dusk is one voice model for Piper. Like every Piper voice, she comes as two files that always go together:

  • The model file (it ends in .onnx). This is the trained neural network itself, the actual voice. It's the big one.
  • The settings file (it ends in .onnx.json). A small text file with her name, language, sample rate, default speed, and the list of speech sounds she knows.

Apps need both files, with matching names, in the same place. When she's finished, the files will be called en_US-dusk-high.onnx and en_US-dusk-high.onnx.json, and apps will just show her as "Dusk".

How she works

Piper voices are built on a kind of neural network called VITS. Getting from your text to her voice takes three steps:

  1. Text becomes speech sounds. A free tool called espeak-ng turns the text into phonemes, the tiny individual sounds of speech. For example, "dusk" becomes four sounds: d, uh, s, k. That's how she knows how to pronounce words.
  2. Speech sounds become audio. Those phonemes go into the neural network (the .onnx file). It figures out how long each sound should last and what it should sound like, and then it creates the audio directly.
  3. Audio comes out. What you get is audio at 22,050 samples per second (22,050 Hz), which your app plays or your screen reader speaks.

Since all of this happens on your own device, Dusk doesn't need the internet at all once she's installed.

Her speech settings

Three settings inside the .onnx.json file shape how she talks:

  • length_scale is her speed. A bigger number is slower and a smaller number is faster. Her default pace is about 144 words per minute, and your screen reader's rate slider can still take her faster or slower from there.
  • noise_scale is how much variation goes into the voice itself. Higher sounds more expressive but can get shaky. Lower sounds steadier and smoother.
  • noise_w is how much variation goes into her timing and rhythm. Higher sounds less robotic, lower sounds more even.

How she's trained

The recordings

Dusk learns from one reader: Celine Major, reader 11697 in the Hi-Fi TTS dataset, which is licensed CC BY 4.0. Her recordings come from LibriVox audiobooks.

I didn't use every bit of her audio. My notebook measures all of her clips and picks about 3,069 of them (around 3.5 hours) that are:

  • Lively. The clips with the widest pitch range, where her voice moves up and down the most.
  • Clean. The noisiest clips, the odd character voices, and the badly aligned clips all get left out.

The clips that make the cut are converted to 22,050 Hz and evened out in loudness.

Where she starts from

Training a voice from zero would take way too long on free computers. So Dusk starts from Piper's public-domain LJ Speech "high" voice, which already knows how to speak English, and training teaches it to sound like Dusk instead. This is called fine-tuning, or warm-starting.

Where she trains, and for how long

  • She trains on Google Colab's free T4 GPU, using the notebook in my repo.
  • She learns from 12 clips at a time (a batch size of 12). I tried 16, but that ran the T4 out of memory.
  • The plan is 110 epochs. One epoch is one pass through all of her clips, and all 110 add up to about 19 hours of GPU time.
  • Colab disconnects free sessions, so she trains in sessions. Checkpoints (saved snapshots of the model) go to Google Drive, and each new session picks up from the newest one.
  • Colab's free GPU time has a limit. When it runs out, training waits until Google gives the GPU back, so finishing her takes several days. Good things take time.

Versions: Compact, Standard and Enhanced

Every version is the same Dusk. They just differ in size and in how long she's trained.

Dusk Compact

  • Made for the iPhone app Piper - Neural TTS.
  • Half precision (fp16), about 58 MB.
  • Also comes split into two parts, an encoder and a decoder, so she uses less memory. iOS gives voice apps very little memory, and a full-size voice can get cut off on long text, which makes VoiceOver fall back to its default voice. Compact avoids that.
  • Files: dusk-compact-ios.zip, with the half-size en_US-dusk-high.onnx, its .onnx.json, plus en_US-dusk-high.encoder.onnx and en_US-dusk-high.decoder.onnx.

Dusk Standard

  • Made for NVDA, Android, Piper on a computer, and Home Assistant.
  • Full precision (fp32), about 110 MB.
  • Files: dusk-standard.zip, with the full-size en_US-dusk-high.onnx and its .onnx.json.

Dusk Enhanced

  • Coming later.
  • The same voice, trained longer.

How I make the Compact files

The Compact files come from a half-precision conversion plus Piper's own encoder and decoder splitter. I tested this recipe on the public LJ Speech "high" voice first, and it matched the Piper iPhone app's own published compact files: same inputs and outputs, and identical audio with randomness turned off. Split voices are new in the iPhone app, though, so some app versions might only accept the half-size .onnx and .onnx.json without the two split files.

Previews

Previews are early test versions I make from a checkpoint partway through training, so I can hear how she's coming along (and so you can too).

Dusk Preview 1

  • Made from the epoch 25 checkpoint.
  • Compact size (fp16, about 58 MB).
  • Files: dusk-preview-1.onnx and dusk-preview-1.onnx.json.
  • The download buttons are up in the Download section.
  • How she sounds: totally understandable, but still raspy and a little shaky. That's normal for a voice this early in training, and it smooths out as she keeps learning.

Why every preview gets its own name

The iPhone app keeps track of voices by the name inside the settings file. If two voices share the same identity, the new one replaces the old one, or the app just keeps the old one loaded. So every preview gets its own name and dataset id. Preview 1 is called "Dusk Preview 1", with the dataset id dusk_preview_1. That way the previews and the finished Dusk can all live side by side on one phone.

Roadmap

  1. Keep training until all 110 epochs are done, then release Dusk Standard and Dusk Compact.
  2. Next build: blend in more openly licensed voices. I'm planning to add recordings from Kristin (public domain, from LibriVox) and HFC Female (licensed CC BY-NC-SA 4.0) alongside Celine Major. The goal is a voice with lively pitch, strong clarity and crisp consonants.
  3. Dusk Enhanced: the same voice, trained longer.
  4. Someday, maybe: submit Dusk to Piper's official voice list. That one's up to the Piper maintainers.

A quick, honest license note about the blended build: if HFC Female's recordings are used, that build's voice files will be CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. That means non-commercial use only, and anything made from them has to use the same license. Builds trained only on Celine Major (and public domain data) stay CC BY 4.0.

Install

The finished files will live on my Dusk releases page on GitHub. Short steps for each app are below, and there are longer notes in the Dusk integration notes.

iPhone (VoiceOver), with Dusk Preview 1

You'll use the free, open-source app Piper - Neural TTS on the App Store. It needs iOS 18.

  1. In Safari, tap the Preview 1 voice model and choose Download. Then do the same with the Preview 1 settings file.
  2. Both files land in the Downloads folder in the Files app. That's in On My iPhone or in iCloud Drive, depending on your Safari settings. If you want them on your phone for sure, set Safari's download location to On My iPhone in Settings, under Safari, Downloads.
  3. Open Piper - Neural TTS, go to Settings, then Import Model from Files.
  4. Open that Downloads folder, select dusk-preview-1.onnx and dusk-preview-1.onnx.json in one pick, and tap Install.
  5. In iPhone Settings, go to Accessibility, VoiceOver, Speech, then Add Voice (or Voice), English, and choose Dusk Preview 1. You can pick her for Spoken Content too.

Tip if the files look dimmed: the import picker only works with real local files in On My iPhone. Files that are still sitting in iCloud Drive, Google Drive, Dropbox or another cloud provider can show up dimmed, or fail to import without saying anything. If that happens, copy them into On My iPhone first (for example: open the file in the cloud app, then Share, Save to Files, On My iPhone). Downloading straight from the buttons in Safari skips this problem.

iPhone (VoiceOver), with Dusk Compact

Once the finished voice is out, it's almost the same:

  1. Download dusk-compact-ios.zip from the releases page and unzip it in the Files app.
  2. Make sure the files are real local files in On My iPhone (see the tip above).
  3. Open Piper - Neural TTS, go to Settings, then Import Model from Files.
  4. Select en_US-dusk-high.onnx and en_US-dusk-high.onnx.json in one pick. If your app version supports split voices, also select en_US-dusk-high.encoder.onnx and en_US-dusk-high.decoder.onnx. Then tap Install.
  5. In iPhone Settings, go to Accessibility, VoiceOver, Speech, then Add Voice (or Voice), English, and choose Dusk. You can pick her for Spoken Content too.

Updating to a newer Dusk? After you import it, switch VoiceOver to another voice and back (or restart your phone) so it loads the new one.

NVDA (Windows), with Dusk Standard

  1. Install the Sonata Neural Voices add-on for NVDA.
  2. Download en_US-dusk-high-nvda-sonata.tar.gz from the releases page.
  3. In NVDA, open Sonata's voice manager, go to the Installed tab, choose "Install from local file", and pick the file.
  4. Restart NVDA, then choose Sonata and Dusk in NVDA's speech settings.

Android, with Dusk Standard

  1. Install SherpaTTS from F-Droid (Android 10 or newer).
  2. Download vits-piper-en_US-dusk-high.tar.bz2 from the releases page and unpack it.
  3. In SherpaTTS, go to Manage Languages, then Install from SD Card. Use the language code eng and the name Dusk, then pick en_US-dusk-high.onnx and tokens.txt from the unpacked folder.
  4. In Android Settings, go to Accessibility, Text-to-speech output, and choose SherpaTTS.

Piper on a computer (Windows, Mac, Linux), with Dusk Standard

Put both Standard files in one folder, then run these two commands:

pip install piper-tts
python3 -m piper -m en_US-dusk-high --data-dir /folder/with/both/files -f test.wav -- "Hi, I'm Dusk."

Home Assistant, with Dusk Standard

Put both Standard files in /share/piper and restart Home Assistant.

JAWS (Windows): not tested yet

JAWS can use Windows SAPI 5 voices, and the Windows text-to-speech bridge for Piper voices is a SAPI 5 engine that runs Piper voices. I haven't confirmed it works with JAWS yet, so I'm calling it untested for now. Steps are in the Dusk integration notes.

Train her yourself

Want to try it? The whole training process lives in one notebook: the Dusk Colab notebook.

  1. Open the notebook in Google Colab.
  2. In the Runtime menu, choose Change runtime type, pick T4 GPU, then Save.
  3. In the Runtime menu, choose Run all (Ctrl+F9). Allow Google Drive access when it asks.
  4. Everything gets saved in one folder in your Google Drive, Dev/Dusk: the data, checkpoints, samples, logs and the finished voice. The notebook doesn't touch anything else in your Drive.
  5. If Colab disconnects or your free GPU time runs out, no worries. Come back later, connect again and press Run all. It skips whatever's already done and picks up from the newest checkpoint.
  6. The Preview cell makes an early test voice from the newest checkpoint and saves it in Dev/Dusk/export/preview-1.
  7. When training's done, the finished voice shows up in Dev/Dusk/export as dusk-standard.zip and dusk-compact-ios.zip.

The Settings cell at the top is the only one you should need to change. And if something breaks, the notebook's last section, "If something goes wrong", covers the common errors, like running out of GPU memory.
